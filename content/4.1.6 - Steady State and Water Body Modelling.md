# 📗 4.1.6 — Steady State and Water Body Modelling

---

## 🎯 Syllabus Statement

> *Demonstrate the steady state of a water body through flow diagrams.*

---

## 📖 Key Concepts

A water body such as a lake, reservoir, or wetland is said to be in **steady state** (or **dynamic equilibrium**) when the total volume of water entering the system equals the total volume leaving it over a given time period. In this condition, the size of the store remains constant even though water is continuously moving through it.

**Inputs** to a lake typically include:
- Direct **precipitation** onto the water surface
- **Surface inflow** (streams and rivers flowing in)
- **Groundwater inflow** (subsurface water entering from surrounding soils)

**Outputs** from a lake typically include:
- **Evaporation** from the water surface
- **Surface outflow** (rivers or streams leaving the lake)
- **Groundwater seepage** (water draining downward into deep aquifers)

When **Σ Inputs = Σ Outputs**, the lake is in steady state. When inputs exceed outputs, the store grows (e.g. during flood years). When outputs exceed inputs, the store shrinks (e.g. during drought or over-extraction).

**Flow diagrams** visualise this balance: a central box (the store) with labelled arrows for each input and output, with numerical values and proportional arrow thickness to indicate flux magnitude.

---

## 📊 Comparisons / Tables

| Condition | Relationship | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| **Steady State** | Inputs = Outputs | Store size unchanged |
| **Positive water balance** | Inputs > Outputs | Store grows (lake rises) |
| **Negative water balance** | Outputs > Inputs | Store shrinks (lake falls) |

**Example — Lake Serene (illustrative):**

| Process | Flux (units/year) |
|---|---|
| Precipitation (input) | 150 |
| Surface inflow (input) | 450 |
| **Total Inputs** | **600** |
| Evaporation (output) | 200 |
| Surface outflow (output) | 380 |
| Groundwater seepage (output) | 20 |
| **Total Outputs** | **600** |

In this example, Inputs (600) = Outputs (600) → **Lake Serene is in steady state.**

---

## 🔍 Case Studies

The steady state concept explains the **Aral Sea crisis (4.1.5)**: when Soviet irrigation projects diverted river inflows, the total inputs to the Aral Sea fell dramatically below total outputs (evaporation continued unchanged), pushing the system into a severe negative water balance. The store shrank by ~90% over 60 years.

---

## ⚖️ Evaluation

The steady state model is useful for understanding whether a water body is stable, growing, or declining, and for predicting the impact of changes to individual flows. However, real water bodies rarely remain in perfect equilibrium — seasonal variation, inter-annual climate fluctuations, and human activities constantly shift the input/output balance. The model is most useful as a baseline against which deviations can be measured and explained.

## 🧠 Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| Steady state | A condition where inputs equal outputs, keeping the store size constant |
| Dynamic equilibrium | A balance maintained through continuous movement rather than a static state |
| Water budget | A quantitative account of inputs, outputs, and store changes in a water system |
| Flow diagram | A diagram using boxes (stores) and arrows (flows) with numerical values to model a system |
| Positive water balance | A condition where inputs exceed outputs; the store grows |
| Negative water balance | A condition where outputs exceed inputs; the store shrinks |
| Open system | A system that exchanges both matter and energy with its surroundings (e.g. most lakes) |
