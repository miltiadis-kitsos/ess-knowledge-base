# 📗 1.2.13–1.2.14 — Models

---

## 🎯 Syllabus Statement

> *1.2.13 A model is a simplified representation of reality; it can be used to understand how a system works and to predict how it will respond to change.*

> *1.2.14 Simplification of a model involves approximation and, therefore, loss of accuracy.*

---

## 📖 Key Concepts

A **model** is any simplified representation of a real system, used to understand how the system functions and to predict its response to change. Models are fundamental tools in ESS because many environmental systems are too complex, large or slow to study directly.

**Types of models used in ESS:**

**Physical models** — scaled-down physical replicas (e.g. Biosphere 2 as a model of Earth's biosphere; a sealed aquarium as a model ecosystem). Tangible and directly manipulable; but very expensive, cannot be scaled up, and emergent failures can be unexpected.

**Conceptual models** — abstract representations using diagrams, flowcharts or systems diagrams (e.g. the carbon cycle diagram; a food web). Simple and widely communicable; clarify structure; but too simplified and cannot produce quantitative predictions.

**Mathematical/computer models** — quantitative simulations using equations and data (e.g. IPCC global climate models; DaisyWorld; the Lotka–Volterra predator–prey model). Allow quantitative predictions and scenario testing; but are dependent on input data quality and cannot include all feedbacks.

All models involve **simplification**: variables are omitted or represented as averages, spatial variation is ignored, feedbacks are linearised. Every simplification introduces **approximation** and, therefore, a degree of inaccuracy. The tension between simplicity and accuracy is fundamental: a simpler model is more tractable and communicable but less representative of reality.

Good scientific practice requires transparency about model limitations — clearly stating what a model includes, excludes and its uncertainty range. The IPCC climate reports present a range of projections from different models rather than a single prediction.

## 🔍 Case Studies

**Global weather forecast models — spatial resolution as approximation**

Weather models divide the atmosphere into a grid — each cell might be 10 km × 10 km. The model calculates temperature, pressure and wind for each cell as if it were uniform. If a school is in a narrow valley 2 km wide, the model cannot "see" it: the valley traps cold air at night, creates its own fog and channels wind differently, but the model uses average conditions across the entire 10 km cell and predicts sunny. Students experience this directly — the app says 22°C and clear, but the valley is foggy and 15°C. The approximation (the grid cell) is necessary — it is impossible to model every square metre of Earth's atmosphere — but it means the model is always wrong at scales smaller than the grid.

## ⚖️ Evaluation

Models are indispensable in ESS, but their limitations must be clearly communicated. A common error is to treat model outputs as reality, rather than as one possible representation with associated uncertainty. Climate models have improved enormously in recent decades and are now reliable enough to underpin international policy (IPCC), but uncertainty ranges remain — particularly for regional projections and feedbacks. The solution is not to abandon models but to use multiple models, compare outputs, and communicate uncertainty ranges honestly. The precautionary principle (taking protective action even under uncertainty) is particularly relevant when models indicate risk of irreversible harm (see 1.2.12 — tipping points).

## 🧠 Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| Model | A simplified representation of a system, used to understand how it functions and to predict its response to change. |
| Simplification | The process of reducing the complexity of a system when building a model by omitting, averaging or linearising variables; involves approximation and some loss of accuracy. |
| Approximation | A value or representation that is close to, but not exactly equal to, the real quantity; inherent in all models. |
| Validation | The process of testing a model against real-world data to assess how accurately it represents the system. |
| Uncertainty | The degree of confidence in a model's predictions; arises from incomplete data, parameter estimation and simplification. |
| Physical model | A tangible, scaled-down replica of a system (e.g. Biosphere 2). |
| Mathematical/computer model | A quantitative simulation of a system using equations and data (e.g. IPCC climate models). |
