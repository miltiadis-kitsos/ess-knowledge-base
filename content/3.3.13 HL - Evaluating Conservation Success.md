# 📗 3.3.13 — Evaluating Conservation Success

---

## 🎯 Syllabus Statement

> *The success of conservation or regeneration measures needs to be assessed.*

---

## 📖 Key Concepts

Conservation success cannot be measured by a single metric. A **triple-level review** is used to assess whether a project has truly succeeded:

1. **Technical success** — Did the measures achieve what was planned ecologically?
2. **Social success** — Was the project well-received by the communities affected?
3. **Strategic success** — Was this the best possible approach to conserving nature?

---

## 📊 Comparisons / Tables

| Level | Question Asked | What to Assess |
|---|---|---|
| **Technical** | Did it work ecologically? | Species recovery, habitat area, ecosystem function |
| **Social** | Was it accepted by local communities? | Employment, cultural impact, community buy-in |
| **Strategic** | Was it the best use of resources? | Cost-effectiveness, opportunity costs, scalability |

---

## 🔍 Case Studies

### Samboja Lestari — East Kalimantan, Borneo

**Background:** In 2001, Willie Smits began restoring 1,800 hectares of fire-damaged, degraded land covered in invasive cogongrass (*Imperata cylindrica*) — effectively a biological desert.

**Methodology:**
- **Soil engineering:** Biochar and compost (with manure and organic waste) used to kickstart microbial life in nutrient-poor clay soils.
- **Succession planting:** Fast-growing "pioneer" species planted first to shade out invasive grasses; then over 1,000 native tree species introduced.
- **The "Rain Machine":** Reforestation reportedly changed the local microclimate — a documented **25% increase in rainfall** and a **3–5°C drop in temperature** within the forest boundaries.

---

### Evaluation — Triple-Level Review

#### 1. Technical Success: Did it work?
**Verdict: High**
- 2,000 ha of degraded grassland converted to functioning secondary rainforest.
- Documented 25% rainfall increase and 3–5°C temperature drop locally.
- Vital sanctuary and gene bank created for hundreds of rehabilitated **Orangutans**.

#### 2. Social Success: Was it well-received?
**Verdict: Mostly Positive**
- Created **3,000+ jobs**, significantly reducing local poverty and crime rates.
- **Sugar palm strategy** provided forest-friendly income 15× higher than traditional farming — turning poachers into protectors.
- Restored the watershed, providing clean water for **30,000 people** in Balikpapan.

#### 3. Strategic Success: Was it the best approach?
**Verdict: Controversial**
- Proved that "dead" land can be restored — a blueprint for millions of hectares of degraded global land.
- **High cost ($2,000+/hectare)** — critics argue funds could be better spent protecting existing primary forests.
- Pushed the land toward a new **alternative stable state**, though long-term management is required to prevent reversion.

---

## ⚖️ Evaluation

Samboja Lestari is a landmark example of what active restoration can achieve in even the most degraded environments. Its social integration model — where conservation directly benefits local communities economically — is considered a key factor in its success. The main critique is **cost-efficiency**: protecting intact primary forest is generally cheaper and more effective per unit of biodiversity gained than restoring degraded land from scratch.

## 🧠 Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| Triple-level review | A framework assessing conservation success technically, socially, and strategically |
| Biochar | Charcoal used as a soil amendment to improve fertility and carbon storage |
| Pioneer species | Fast-growing species that colonise bare or degraded ground, enabling later succession |
| Alternative stable state | A new ecological equilibrium different from the original, triggered by intervention |
| Cogongrass | Invasive grass (*Imperata cylindrica*) that dominates degraded tropical land |
