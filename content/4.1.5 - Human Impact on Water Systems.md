# 📗 4.1.5 — Human Impact on Water Systems

---

## 🎯 Syllabus Statement

> *Human activities can alter the flows and stores of the hydrological cycle.*

---

## 📖 Key Concepts

Human activities fundamentally alter the hydrological cycle by increasing or decreasing specific flows and changing the volume of water held in stores. The four main human pressures on water systems are **agriculture**, **deforestation**, **urbanisation**, and **dams/reservoirs**. Each disrupts the natural balance between stores and flows in distinct ways, with cascading ecological and social consequences.

**Agriculture** is the largest consumer of freshwater globally, primarily through **irrigation**. Withdrawing water from rivers and groundwater at rates that exceed natural recharge depletes stores and reduces downstream flows. Pollution from fertilisers and pesticides adds nutrients to water bodies, causing **eutrophication** — a process that reduces oxygen availability and kills aquatic life.

**Deforestation** removes the forest canopy and root systems that regulate water movement. With fewer trees, transpiration decreases (reducing the transformation flow and lowering atmospheric moisture), while the loss of root systems reduces infiltration capacity, causing faster and more intense surface run-off. This leads to flooding, erosion, and reduced groundwater recharge.

**Urbanisation** replaces permeable natural surfaces with impermeable materials (concrete, asphalt), which prevents infiltration. Rainwater instead becomes rapid surface run-off, moving quickly through drainage systems into rivers. This creates **flashy hydrographs** — sharp spikes in river discharge — increasing flood risk in urban areas.

**Dams and reservoirs** create new surface water stores, providing water security and hydroelectric power, but they alter sediment transport, reduce downstream flow, and can displace communities and ecosystems.

## 📊 Comparisons / Tables

| Human Activity | Flow Increased | Flow Decreased | Store Affected |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Irrigation** | Withdrawal/abstraction | Streamflow, groundwater recharge | Depletes groundwater and rivers |
| **Deforestation** | Surface run-off | Transpiration, infiltration | Reduces groundwater; increases flood risk |
| **Urbanisation** | Surface run-off | Infiltration, groundwater recharge | Reduces groundwater store |
| **Dams** | Evaporation from reservoir | Streamflow downstream | Creates new surface store |

## 🔍 Case Studies

### Case Study 1: Aral Sea — Agriculture and Irrigation (Central Asia)

**Location:** Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan, Central Asia.

**Cause:** Beginning in the 1960s, the Soviet Union initiated massive irrigation projects, diverting water from the Amu Darya and Syr Darya rivers — the Aral Sea's primary inflows — to grow cotton in the surrounding desert.

**Impact on stores:** The Aral Sea's volume has **shrunk by approximately 90%** since 1960, making it one of the most dramatic human-caused hydrological disasters in history.

**Consequences:**
- Collapse of the local fishing industry (the sea once supported a thriving commercial fishery).
- Exposure of the former seabed, creating **toxic dust storms** carrying salt and pesticide residues that cause respiratory illness across the region.
- Extreme seasonal temperature shifts due to the loss of the moderating influence of a large water body.

**Hydrological lesson:** Prioritising one flow (irrigation withdrawal) over the natural balance of inflows and outflows caused an irreversible collapse of a major water store.

---

### Case Study 2: Deforestation and Water Systems — Amazon Rainforest

**Context:** The Amazon rainforest is one of the world's largest hydrological systems. Trees recycle vast quantities of water through transpiration, creating "flying rivers" — atmospheric moisture streams that distribute precipitation across the continent.

**Cause:** Large-scale conversion of rainforest to pastureland and farmland through deforestation.

**Hydrological impact:**
- **Reduced transpiration:** Removing forest canopy drastically reduces this transformation flow, lowering regional atmospheric moisture and precipitation.
- **Increased surface run-off:** Loss of root systems and leaf litter reduces infiltration capacity, causing faster, more intense run-off after rainfall events.

**Consequences:**
- Longer, more severe **droughts** as reduced transpiration leads to less regional rainfall.
- More frequent and intense **flooding and erosion** from flashy run-off, degrading water quality through increased sediment load.

**Tipping point concern:** Scientists fear that continued deforestation may push the Amazon past a **tipping point**, where it transitions from moist rainforest to a drier savanna-like ecosystem — fundamentally altering both the global hydrological and carbon cycles.

---

### Case Study 3: Urbanisation and Run-off — London, UK

**Context:** London is one of Europe's largest cities, with continuous development replacing permeable soil and vegetation with impermeable surfaces (roads, buildings, pavements).

**Hydrological impact:**
- **Reduced infiltration:** Concrete and asphalt prevent rainfall from soaking into the ground, cutting off the primary mechanism for groundwater recharge.
- **Increased surface run-off:** Rainwater moves rapidly into storm drains rather than infiltrating, moving much faster than in natural systems.

**Consequences:**
- **Flash flooding:** Rapid water movement creates sharp, high discharge peaks ("flashy hydrographs") in local rivers, overwhelming outdated sewage and drainage infrastructure.
- **Water quality degradation:** Fast run-off carries oils, heavy metals, and litter directly into waterways without natural filtration.

**Mitigation:** London is increasingly adopting **Sustainable Drainage Systems (SuDS)** — green roofs, permeable paving, and rain gardens — designed to slow surface run-off and promote localised infiltration, mimicking natural hydrological processes.

---

## ⚖️ Evaluation

Human impacts on water systems demonstrate that even large-scale stores (like the Aral Sea) can be critically depleted within decades when flows are mismanaged. The three case studies above show that the consequences are rarely limited to hydrology alone — they cascade into food security, public health, climate regulation, and biodiversity. Solutions exist (SuDS, reforestation, sustainable irrigation) but require political will and long-term investment. The Aral Sea case in particular illustrates that some hydrological changes may be effectively irreversible on human timescales.

## 🧠 Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| Irrigation | The artificial supply of water to land for agriculture; the largest human use of freshwater |
| Eutrophication | Excessive nutrient enrichment of water bodies, causing algal blooms and oxygen depletion |
| Deforestation | The large-scale removal of forest, disrupting transpiration and infiltration |
| Transpiration | Water vapour released from plant leaves — a key hydrological flow in forested landscapes |
| Urbanisation | The expansion of cities and towns, increasing impermeable surfaces |
| Infiltration | Downward movement of water into soil; reduced by urbanisation and deforestation |
| Flashy hydrograph | A river discharge pattern with a rapid, sharp peak caused by fast surface run-off |
| SuDS | Sustainable Drainage Systems — urban infrastructure designed to slow run-off and promote infiltration |
| Tipping point | A threshold beyond which a system shifts irreversibly to a new stable state |
