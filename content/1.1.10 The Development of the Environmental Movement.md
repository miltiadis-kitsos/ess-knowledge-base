# 📗 1.1.10 — The Development of the Environmental Movement

---

## 🎯 Syllabus Statement

> *The development of the environmental movement has been influenced by individuals, literature, the media, major environmental disasters, international agreements, new technologies and scientific discoveries.*

---

## 📖 Key Concepts

The modern environmental movement is generally traced to the latter half of the twentieth century, though its roots extend much further — the Romans recorded concerns about air and water pollution, and soil conservation was practised in China, India and Peru roughly two thousand years ago.

You should be able to give at least one named example under each of the **seven influence categories**: individuals, literature, the media, major environmental disasters, international agreements, new technologies, and scientific discoveries.

## 🔍 Case Studies

### 👤 Individuals



<img src="Carlson.png" align="left" width="300" style="margin-right: 15px"/>
**Rachel Carson (1907–1964)**
Marine biologist whose 1962 book *Silent Spring* exposed how DDT and synthetic pesticides were moving through food chains, devastating wildlife and contaminating water supplies. 
Her rigorous science withstood fierce attacks from the chemical industry and prompted President Kennedy to convene a review of pesticide policy. 
She directly catalysed the creation of the US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) in 1970 and the federal ban on DDT in 1972. 
Carson shifted public understanding of ecology, demonstrating that nature operates as an interconnected web, not a backdrop for human exploitation.


<img src="Maathai.png" align="left" width="300" style="margin-right: 15px"/>
**Wangari Maathai (1940–2011)**
Kenyan academic and activist who founded the **Green Belt Movement** in 1977, mobilising rural women to plant trees on degraded land across Africa — over 51 million trees planted. She pioneered the connection between environmental degradation, poverty and political oppression, broadening the environmental movement beyond its largely Western, middle-class origins. Maathai recognised that deforestation in Kenya was a symptom of corruption and colonial land policies, and linked conservation explicitly to women's rights and civic empowerment. She was awarded the **Nobel Peace Prize in 2004** — the first African woman to receive it — with the committee explicitly citing links between environmental sustainability, peace and good governance.

### 💥 Major Environmental Disasters
<img src="Chernobyl.png" align="left" width="300" style="margin-right: 15px"/>
**Chernobyl Nuclear Disaster (Ukraine, 1986)**
On 26 April 1986, reactor No. 4 at the Chernobyl Nuclear Power Plant exploded during a safety test, releasing **400 times more radiation than the Hiroshima bomb** and contaminating over **200,000 km²** across the Soviet Union and Europe.

The immediate evacuation of 350,000 people created the 30 km Exclusion Zone; Pripyat was abandoned overnight and remains uninhabited. 

Long-term consequences included severe contamination of soil, rivers, and the food chain, with elevated mutation rates and cancer incidence in wildlife. The disaster exposed the dangers of secrecy in nuclear governance, accelerated the fall of the Soviet Union by eroding public trust, and directly shaped global nuclear safety regulations.


<img src="Bhopal.png" align="left" width="450" style="margin-right: 12px"/>
**Bhopal Gas Tragedy (India, 1984)**
On the night of 2–3 December 1984, ✱Union Carbide pesticide plant in Bhopal released over **40 tonnes of toxic methyl isocyanate (MIC)** gas into a densely populated city, killing an estimated **15,000–20,000 people** and injuring over **500,000** — the world's worst industrial disaster. 

Survivors suffered chronic respiratory disease, neurological damage, immune disorders, and elevated rates of cancer and birth defects across generations. The site was never fully decontaminated; toxic waste continues to leach into local groundwater.

Bhopal galvanised the global movement for corporate accountability, the right to know about industrial hazards, and stricter environmental justice standards, and exposed the dangers of exporting hazardous industrial processes to countries with weaker safety regulation.

### 🌍 International Agreements


<img src="Stockholm.png" align="left" width="450" style="margin-right: 12px"/>
**Stockholm Conference (Sweden, 1972)**
The first major UN conference dedicated to environmental issues, attended by representatives from **122 nations**; it placed the environment on the international political agenda for the first time and established 5 June as **World Environment Day**. 
It produced the **Stockholm Declaration** — 26 principles affirming states' sovereign right to exploit their own resources but also their responsibility not to cause environmental damage beyond their borders. 
It led directly to the creation of the **United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP)**, headquartered in Nairobi — the first UN agency based in a developing country. Indian PM Indira Gandhi's landmark speech argued that poverty is the greatest polluter, linking ecological health to social justice in a way that shaped all subsequent international environmental negotiations.


<img src="Rio.png" align="left" width="450" style="margin-right: 12px"/>
**Rio Earth Summit (Brazil, 1992)**
The largest environmental conference in history to that point, attended by **108 heads of state and 172 governments**; it popularised **sustainable development** as the guiding framework for global policy. It established the **UNFCCC** — the legal foundation for the Kyoto Protocol (1997) and the Paris Agreement (2015) — and opened the **Convention on Biological Diversity** for signature. 

It produced **Agenda 21**, a comprehensive 300-page action plan for sustainable development, and the **Rio Declaration** — 27 principles including the **precautionary principle** and the **polluter pays principle** that continue to underpin environmental law worldwide.

It formally recognised the role of NGOs in environmental governance and established the principle of **"common but differentiated responsibilities."**

### 🌾 New Technologies / Scientific Discoveries

**The Green Revolution (1940s–1970s)**
A period of intensive agricultural transformation led by scientists including **Norman Borlaug**, involving the development and mass dissemination of **high-yielding variety (HYV)** seeds for wheat, rice and maize — combined with synthetic fertilisers, pesticides and irrigation — that dramatically increased crop yields across South Asia, Latin America and parts of Africa. Between 1960 and 1985 cereal production in developing countries more than doubled; India became self-sufficient in grain production. Borlaug was awarded the **Nobel Peace Prize in 1970**. However, it carried severe environmental costs: heavy reliance on synthetic nitrogen fertilisers led to soil degradation, eutrophication of waterways, and loss of soil biodiversity; monoculture farming eroded genetic diversity; and intensive groundwater extraction depleted aquifers. Its legacy directly informs ESS discussions on ecosystem services, food systems and the limits of technological fixes.

## ⚖️ Evaluation

The seven influence categories show that the environmental movement was not driven by any single force but by a convergence of individual action, scientific evidence, media coverage, catastrophic events and political will. Disasters like Chernobyl and Bhopal were important in demonstrating the real-world cost of ignoring environmental risk, but their influence was amplified by media coverage and NGO campaigns. International agreements like Stockholm and Rio created frameworks, but their effectiveness depended on national implementation — a persistent weakness of voluntary international environmental governance.

## 🧠 Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| Environmental movement | The worldwide campaign to raise awareness and coordinate action to address the negative effects of human activity on the planet. |
| Silent Spring | Rachel Carson's 1962 book exposing the harmful effects of synthetic pesticides on ecosystems, widely credited with launching the modern environmental movement. |
| Green Belt Movement | The organisation founded by Wangari Maathai in 1977 to plant trees and empower rural women across Africa. |
| Stockholm Conference (1972) | The UN Conference on the Human Environment — the first major international conference on environmental issues. |
| UNEP | The United Nations Environment Programme, established after the Stockholm Conference, headquartered in Nairobi. |
| Rio Earth Summit (1992) | The UN Conference on Environment and Development; produced Agenda 21, the Rio Declaration, and the UNFCCC. |
| UNFCCC | The United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change, established at Rio 1992 — the legal foundation for all subsequent climate agreements. |
| Precautionary principle | The principle that where there is a threat of serious or irreversible environmental damage, lack of full scientific certainty should not be used as a reason for postponing measures to prevent damage. |
| Polluter pays principle | The principle that those responsible for producing pollution should bear the costs of managing it. |
| Green Revolution | The period from the 1940s–1970s marked by major increases in agricultural productivity through HYV seeds, fertilisers, pesticides and irrigation. |
| High-yielding variety (HYV) | Crop varieties selectively bred to produce significantly higher yields than traditional varieties when grown with sufficient inputs. |
| Common but differentiated responsibilities | The Rio principle that all states share responsibility for global environmental problems, but wealthier nations bear greater historical responsibility. |
