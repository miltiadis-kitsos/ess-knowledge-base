# 📗 1.2.10–1.2.12 — Positive Feedback and Tipping Points

---

## 🎯 Syllabus Statement

> *1.2.10 Positive feedback loops occur when a disturbance leads to an amplification of that disturbance, destabilizing the system and driving it away from its equilibrium.*

> *1.2.11 Positive feedback loops will tend to drive the system toward a tipping point.*

> *1.2.12 Tipping points can exist within a system where a small alteration in one component can produce large overall changes, resulting in a shift in equilibrium.*

---

## 📖 Key Concepts

A **positive feedback loop** occurs when the output of a process amplifies the original disturbance rather than counteracting it, driving the system progressively further from its original equilibrium. Positive feedback is inherently **destabilising** — it acts as an amplifier, reinforcing change rather than correcting it.

A **tipping point** (or critical threshold) is the point at which a small additional change is sufficient to drive the system irreversibly to a qualitatively different, new equilibrium. Tipping points are difficult to predict because systems may appear stable while positive feedbacks are accumulating beneath the surface. The shift can appear sudden relative to the slow accumulation of pressure.

**Contrast:** Negative feedback = thermostat (self-correcting). Positive feedback = amplifier (self-reinforcing).

**Key example — Arctic ice-albedo feedback:** As global temperatures rise → Arctic sea ice melts → dark ocean exposed → lower albedo → more solar radiation absorbed → further warming → more ice melts.

**Another example — permafrost-carbon feedback:** Rising temperatures → permafrost thaws → stored CH₄ and CO₂ released → greenhouse effect strengthened → further warming → more permafrost thaws.

## 🔍 Case Studies

**Amazon Rainforest tipping point (~5.5 million km²)**

The Amazon rainforest is the world's largest tropical rainforest, spanning nine countries and containing approximately 10% of all species on Earth. It stores an estimated 150–200 Pg (petagrams) of carbon and generates approximately 50–75% of its own rainfall through moisture recycling via evapotranspiration — the "flying rivers" of the Amazon.

Scientists Thomas Lovejoy and Carlos Nobre proposed a tipping point threshold of approximately **20–25% deforestation** of the original forest cover. Beyond this point, the self-sustaining moisture recycling system fails: reduced evapotranspiration → less rainfall → forest die-back → less evapotranspiration → further die-back (a positive feedback). As of the early 2020s, approximately **17–20%** of the original Amazon has been deforested. Some regions have already crossed local tipping points and are now emitting more CO₂ than they absorb — a reversal of the forest's role as a carbon sink. The projected shift would be from tropical rainforest to degraded savanna (**savannification**).

---

## ⚖️ Evaluation

Tipping points are among the most dangerous environmental phenomena because they may be crossed before we recognise them. The non-linear response of systems near tipping points means that small changes in pressure can have disproportionately large consequences — making precautionary management essential. However, identifying tipping points in advance is technically very difficult: models may not capture all feedbacks, and there may be no reliable early warning signal. Positive feedback does not always produce harmful outcomes: in social and technological systems, positive feedback in pro-environmental behaviour (e.g. adoption of renewable energy driving down costs, driving further adoption) can be highly desirable.

## 🧠 Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| Positive feedback loop | A feedback mechanism in which the output of a process amplifies the original disturbance, driving the system progressively further from its equilibrium. |
| Tipping point | A threshold in a system beyond which a small additional change produces large, often irreversible shifts to a new equilibrium. |
| Albedo | A measure of the reflectivity of a surface; the proportion of incoming solar radiation reflected rather than absorbed. Ranges from 0 to 1. |
| Permafrost | Ground that has been continuously frozen for at least two years; stores large quantities of organic carbon that may be released as CO₂ and CH₄ upon thawing. |
| Savannification | The conversion of tropical rainforest to savanna or degraded grassland; a potential tipping point outcome for the Amazon. |
| Ice-albedo feedback | Positive feedback in which melting sea ice exposes darker ocean, reducing albedo and causing further warming and ice loss. |
