## 🔑 Key Cross-Links

**Organisations → Evaluation:**
[[3.3.10 HL - Conservation Organisations and Success Factors]] identifies the *actors* (who funds and runs conservation); [[3.3.13 HL - Evaluating Conservation Success]] provides the *framework* for assessing whether their work succeeds. In a Paper 2 response, always identify the type of organisation involved before applying the triple-level review.

**Rewilding as positive feedback:**
[[3.3.11 HL - Rewilding and Positive Feedback Loops]] applies 1.2.10–1.2.12 directly — rewilding is a *deliberate* trigger for a positive feedback loop that pushes the ecosystem toward a new, higher-biodiversity alternative stable state. This is the opposite of the *harmful* positive feedbacks (Amazon deforestation, ice-albedo) met in 1.2.

**Rewilding theory → Rewilding practice:**
[[3.3.11 HL - Rewilding and Positive Feedback Loops]] (Yellowstone — large, intact, predator-led) and [[3.3.12 HL- Rewilding Benefits and Limitations]] (Knepp — small, agricultural, herbivore proxy-led) are two ends of the rewilding spectrum. Comparing them reveals how scale and context determine outcomes.

**Ecotourism → Conservation funding:**
[[3.3.14 HL - Ecotourism Benefits and Impacts]] connects to [[3.3.10 HL - Conservation Organisations and Success Factors]] — ecotourism revenue can substitute for or supplement NGO/government conservation budgets. The Galápagos National Park entrance fees directly fund the conservation work that IGOs and NGOs would otherwise have to finance.

**Strategic success → Ecotourism:**
The *strategic* level of [[3.3.13 HL - Evaluating Conservation Success]] asks "was this the best use of resources?" — ecotourism (3.3.14) is one answer: it generates sustainable revenue from the ecosystem itself, avoiding dependence on external funding.
