# 📗 1.2.8–1.2.9 — Negative Feedback and Equilibrium

---

## 🎯 Syllabus Statement

> *1.2.8 Negative feedback loops occur when the output of a process inhibits or reverses the operation of the same process in such a way as to reduce change. They are stabilizing as they counteract deviation.*

> *1.2.9 As an open system, an ecosystem will normally exist in a stable equilibrium, either in a steady-state equilibrium or in one developing over time (for example, succession), and will be maintained by stabilizing negative feedback loops.*

---

## 📖 Key Concepts

A **negative feedback loop** occurs when the output of a process feeds back to inhibit or reverse that same process, counteracting the initial change and returning the system toward its original state. Negative feedback is inherently **stabilising** — it acts like a thermostat, resisting deviation and promoting equilibrium.

A **steady-state equilibrium** is the condition of an open system in which flows are still occurring but inputs are constantly balanced with outputs; there is no net change over the long term, though short-term oscillations exist. A **dynamic equilibrium** is one in which the system fluctuates or oscillates around a central value over time, maintained by ongoing negative feedback processes — inputs and outputs are not continuously balanced at any given moment, but net change remains minimal over the long term.

Ecosystems normally exist in one of these two forms of stable equilibrium, maintained by negative feedback loops. Succession (the gradual change of an ecosystem over time toward a climax community) represents a system in a developing equilibrium — the system is changing, but in an orderly, directional way.

## 🔍 Case Studies

**Snowshoe Hare and Canadian Lynx, Northern Canada (Hudson Bay region, ~1850–1925)**

The Hudson Bay Trading Company maintained pelt (fur) records over approximately 100 years, providing one of the longest ecological time series in existence. The records show cyclical fluctuations in snowshoe hare (*Lepus americanus*) and Canadian lynx (*Lynx canadensis*) populations with a period of approximately **9–10 years**.

Because the lynx is the near-sole predator of the hare and the hare is the primary prey of the lynx, this system is a near-perfect two-species negative feedback loop. The lynx population always **lags behind** the hare population by approximately 1–2 years because predators must feed, survive and reproduce before their population responds to the change in prey density. **Time lags** are a characteristic feature of ecological feedback loops and can cause the system to oscillate rather than return smoothly to equilibrium (see 1.2.17).

## ⚖️ Evaluation

Negative feedback is the fundamental mechanism of ecological stability and is why ecosystems are often resilient to small disturbances. However, negative feedback only operates effectively when disturbances are within the system's buffering capacity. Large, rapid disturbances (e.g. sudden temperature increase, major pollution event) can overwhelm negative feedback mechanisms, pushing the system past a threshold where positive feedback takes over (see 1.2.10–1.2.12). The lynx-hare system, while a powerful illustration, is also a simplification — real predator-prey systems involve many species, and the hare's decline is also partly driven by food availability, not just predation, complicating the clean feedback narrative.
