# 📗 1.2.1–1.2.3 — What Is a System and System Diagrams

---

## 🎯 Syllabus Statement

> *1.2.1 Systems are sets of interacting or interdependent components.*

> *1.2.2 A systems approach is a holistic way of visualizing a complex set of interactions, and it can be applied to ecological or societal situations.*

> *1.2.3 In system diagrams, storages are usually represented as rectangular boxes and flows as arrows, with the direction of each arrow indicating the direction of each flow.*

---

## 📖 Key Concepts

A system is an assemblage of interacting or interdependent parts and the relationships between them that together constitute a functional whole. Components are connected through the transfer of energy and matter, with all parts linked and affecting each other.

There are two broad approaches to studying systems. The **reductionist approach** divides the system into its component parts and studies each separately — the traditional method in experimental science. The **holistic approach** studies the system as a whole, describing patterns and processes at the level of the entire system; this is used in modern ecological investigation. The systems approach emphasises similarities in the way that matter, energy and information flow across disciplines, integrating ecology, sociology, economics and politics into a unified analytical framework.

A system consists of **storages** (places where matter or energy is held) and **flows** (processes by which matter or energy enters or leaves a storage). These concepts apply equally to ecological systems (e.g. an ecosystem) and societal systems (e.g. a political or economic system).

Systems diagrams are visual representations that allow complex systems to be mapped and communicated. They show the structure of a system — its components, storages, flows, inputs and outputs — in a standardised format. By convention: storages = rectangular boxes; flows = arrows (arrowhead indicates direction). Processes driving each flow (e.g. photosynthesis, respiration, diffusion, decomposition) are typically labelled on or beside the arrows. The size of boxes and arrows can be drawn proportionally to represent the magnitude of the storage or flow.

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| System | An assemblage of interacting or interdependent components and the relationships between them that together constitute a functional whole. |
| Holistic approach | The study of a system as a whole, focusing on patterns and processes at the level of the entire system rather than on individual components. |
| Reductionist approach | An approach that divides a system into parts and studies each component separately; the traditional method of experimental science. |
| Storage (store) | A place within a system where matter or energy is held or retained. |
| Flow | A process by which matter or energy enters, moves through or leaves a system's storages. |
| Systems diagram | A visual representation of a system showing storages as boxes and flows as arrows, used to model inputs, outputs and processes within a system. |
| Input | Matter or energy entering a system from outside its boundary. |
| Output | Matter or energy leaving a system across its boundary. |
| Boundary | A line that defines the limits of a system, distinguishing what is inside from what is outside. |
| Process | The mechanism by which a flow (transfer or transformation) occurs between two storages within a system. |

## ⚖️ Evaluation

The systems approach is powerful because it reveals emergent properties and feedbacks that reductionism misses. However, choosing where to draw a system's boundary is inherently subjective — a small change in boundary definition can dramatically alter what is included as an input or output. Systems diagrams are excellent communication tools but can oversimplify very complex dynamics: labelling an arrow "respiration" does not capture the full biochemical complexity of that process. Their value lies in revealing structure and connectivity, not in capturing mechanism.
