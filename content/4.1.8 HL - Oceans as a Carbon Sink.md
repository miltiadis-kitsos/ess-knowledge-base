# 📗 4.1.8 HL — Oceans as a Carbon Sink

---

## 🎯 Syllabus Statement

> *Explain how oceans act as a carbon sink and the process of acidification.*

---

## 📖 Key Concepts

Oceans are the largest active carbon sink on Earth, absorbing between **25–31% of anthropogenic CO₂** emitted annually. This absorption occurs through two distinct mechanisms that operate on very different timescales: the **solubility pump** (short-term chemical absorption) and the **biological pump** (long-term biological sequestration).

**Short-term — Solubility Pump and Acidification:**
When CO₂ dissolves in seawater, it reacts with water (H₂O) to form **carbonic acid (H₂CO₃)**, which then dissociates to release hydrogen ions (H⁺). The increase in H⁺ ions lowers ocean **pH** — a process called **ocean acidification**. Since the Industrial Revolution, the average ocean pH has dropped from approximately 8.2 to 8.1 — a seemingly small change that represents a **26% increase in acidity** (due to the logarithmic pH scale). This threatens marine organisms that build calcium carbonate shells and skeletons (e.g. corals, oysters, pteropods), as more acidic water dissolves these structures.

**Long-term — Biological Pump:**
Marine phytoplankton absorb dissolved CO₂ during photosynthesis, converting it into **organic biomass**. When these organisms die, their carbon-rich remains (shells, skeletons, organic matter) sink to the seafloor and are buried as **sediment**, effectively removing carbon from the active carbon cycle for millions of years. This is the mechanism by which fossil fuels originally formed.

---

## 📊 Comparisons / Tables

| Mechanism | Timescale | Process | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Solubility Pump** | Short-term (years–decades) | CO₂ dissolves in seawater → carbonic acid forms → pH decreases | Ocean acidification; threatens shell-forming organisms |
| **Biological Pump** | Long-term (thousands–millions of years) | Phytoplankton fix CO₂ → organisms die → carbon buried in sediment | Long-term carbon sequestration |

**Chemical equation (acidification):**
CO₂ + H₂O → H₂CO₃ → H⁺ + HCO₃⁻

## 🔍 Case Studies

**Great Barrier Reef, Australia:**
Ocean acidification (combined with warming) is one of the leading causes of **coral bleaching** on the Great Barrier Reef. As pH falls, the ability of corals to calcify (build their calcium carbonate skeletons) is impaired. This weakens reef structure, reduces habitat availability for thousands of species, and threatens the reef's long-term survival.

---

## ⚖️ Evaluation

The ocean's role as a carbon sink has been vital in slowing the rate of atmospheric CO₂ accumulation and thus limiting the pace of climate change. However, this "service" comes at a cost: as more CO₂ is absorbed, acidification intensifies, threatening marine biodiversity and the very biological pump that enables long-term sequestration. There is a risk that as ocean warming reduces phytoplankton productivity and acidification weakens shell-forming organisms, the ocean's capacity to act as a carbon sink could diminish — creating a dangerous positive feedback with climate change.

## 🧠 Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| Carbon sink | A system that absorbs more carbon than it releases (e.g. oceans, forests) |
| Ocean acidification | The decrease in ocean pH caused by absorption of atmospheric CO₂ |
| Carbonic acid | H₂CO₃ — formed when CO₂ dissolves in water; lowers pH |
| Biological pump | The process by which marine organisms fix CO₂ into biomass that is eventually buried as seafloor sediment |
| Solubility pump | The chemical absorption of CO₂ into seawater driven by differences in CO₂ concentration |
| Phytoplankton | Microscopic marine photosynthesisers that fix CO₂ and form the base of marine food webs |
| Calcification | The biological process of building structures from calcium carbonate; impaired by ocean acidification |
