# 📗 4.1.9 HL — Ocean Stratification

---

## 🎯 Syllabus Statement

> *Explain the causes and consequences of ocean stratification.*

---

## 📖 Key Concepts

**Ocean stratification** refers to the layering of ocean water into distinct horizontal zones that resist mixing. This stratification arises because water density varies with both **temperature** and **salinity** — warmer, less saline water is less dense and sits above colder, saltier, denser water.

**Causes of Stratification:**
- **Temperature:** Solar radiation warms the surface layer, making it less dense than the cold deep water below.
- **Salinity:** Evaporation at the surface increases salinity, but in regions of high rainfall or ice melt, surface water may be fresher (less dense) than deep water.
- **Seasonal variation:** Stratification is strongest in summer (when surface heating is intense) and weakest in winter (when surface cooling reduces the density difference).

**The Thermocline:**
The **thermocline** is the boundary layer between the warm, well-lit surface layer and the cold, dark deep ocean. It is characterised by a rapid decrease in temperature with depth. The thermocline acts as a barrier that inhibits vertical mixing of water between layers.

**Consequences of Stratification:**
When stratification is strong, nutrient-rich deep water cannot mix upward into the sunlit surface layer (the **photic zone**). Since phytoplankton require both sunlight (available only near the surface) and nutrients (concentrated in the deep), strong stratification reduces **marine productivity**. This is why highly stratified tropical oceans are often described as "marine deserts" — despite warm, clear water, low nutrient availability limits phytoplankton growth and the food webs that depend on it.

Climate change is intensifying ocean stratification by warming surface waters, which increases the density gradient between layers and further limits vertical mixing.

## 📊 Comparisons / Tables

| Layer | Characteristics | Productivity |
|---|---|---|
| **Surface layer (photic zone)** | Warm, well-lit, low nutrients | Limited by nutrients |
| **Thermocline** | Rapid temperature drop; density barrier | Transition zone |
| **Deep ocean** | Cold, dark, nutrient-rich | Limited by light |

| Factor | Effect on Stratification |
|---|---|
| **Increased surface warming** | Stronger stratification; less mixing |
| **Wind and storms** | Weaken stratification; promote mixing |
| **Upwellings** | Break stratification locally; bring nutrients to surface |

## 🔍 Case Studies

**Tropical vs. Polar Ocean Productivity:**
Highly stratified tropical oceans (e.g. central Pacific) are nutrient-poor and relatively unproductive. In contrast, polar regions and areas of active upwelling (e.g. the Humboldt Current off Peru) are less stratified, allowing nutrients to reach the surface — supporting some of the world's most productive fisheries.

**Climate Change Impact:**
Studies show that as global temperatures rise, surface ocean warming is increasing stratification globally, reducing nutrient mixing and threatening the productivity of marine ecosystems and the fisheries that support billions of people.

---

## ⚖️ Evaluation

Ocean stratification represents a fundamental challenge for marine productivity: the resources needed by phytoplankton (light and nutrients) are found in different layers, and stratification prevents them from coming together. While this is a natural condition, climate change is intensifying stratification, potentially pushing marine ecosystems toward lower productivity. This has implications not only for biodiversity but also for the ocean's role as a carbon sink — reduced phytoplankton productivity weakens the biological pump (4.1.8).

## 🧠 Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| Ocean stratification | The layering of ocean water into distinct zones of different density that resist mixing |
| Thermocline | The boundary layer between warm surface water and cold deep water, marked by rapid temperature decrease with depth |
| Photic zone | The surface layer of the ocean where sufficient light penetrates for photosynthesis |
| Marine productivity | The rate at which organic matter is produced by marine organisms, particularly phytoplankton |
| Density | The mass per unit volume of water; increases with decreasing temperature and increasing salinity |
| Upwelling | The vertical movement of cold, nutrient-rich deep water to the surface, breaking stratification locally |
