# 📗 1.1.8 — Technocentric, Anthropocentric and Ecocentric Worldviews

---

## 🎯 Syllabus Statement

> *Environmental perspectives (worldviews) can be classified into the broad categories of technocentric, anthropocentric and ecocentric.*

---

## 📖 Key Concepts

Environmental worldviews are commonly grouped into three broad categories, forming a **spectrum** rather than three rigid boxes:

**Ecocentric** worldviews place nature and ecological integrity at the centre of concern. Ecocentrists favour small-scale, low-technology lifestyles and emphasise restraint in resource use. The most extreme ecocentrists are called **deep ecologists**, who argue that nature has intrinsic value independent of human interests.

**Technocentric** worldviews place confidence in technology and human ingenuity to solve environmental problems, and generally support continued economic growth. At the most optimistic extreme, technocentrists are sometimes labelled **cornucopians** — they believe human resourcefulness is essentially unlimited.

**Anthropocentric** worldviews sit conceptually between the two: humans are placed at the centre, but the priority is to *sustainably manage* the global system through mechanisms such as taxation, regulation and legislation. This is generally a pragmatic, consensus-based approach.

## 🔍 Case Studies

**Carbon Emissions & Water Scarcity — Global Examples**

*Carbon dioxide emissions:*
- Technocentric (cornucopian): technological fixes such as carbon capture and storage (CCS) allow continued fossil fuel use.
- Ecocentric: reduce emissions at source by curtailing fossil-fuel-based industry, even at some cost to economic growth.

*Water scarcity:*
- Technocentric:** engineering solutions such as desalination or cloud seeding increase supply.
- Ecocentric: conservation, recycling, and monitoring keep use within sustainable limits.

In both cases, the technocentric approach seeks to maintain existing consumption patterns by increasing supply or capturing emissions through innovation, while the ecocentric approach seeks to reduce demand and restore natural balance.

## ⚖️ Evaluation

The three-category spectrum is a useful heuristic for ESS discussions, but real-world positions rarely fall neatly into one category. Most environmental policies blend elements of all three. Additionally, the labels carry implicit value judgements: "cornucopian" is often used dismissively, and "deep ecologist" can sound extreme, which can distort analysis. Students should apply the categories descriptively rather than normatively.

## 🧠 Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| Ecocentrism | A nature-centred worldview placing ecological integrity and the intrinsic value of nature at the centre, favouring restraint and low-technology approaches. |
| Anthropocentrism | In ESS, a worldview in which humans must sustainably manage the global system, typically through regulation, taxation and legislation. |
| Technocentrism | A technology-centred worldview holding that technological development can resolve environmental problems, generally supporting continued economic growth. |
| Deep ecologist | An individual at the extreme ecocentric end of the spectrum, valuing nature intrinsically above human interests. |
| Cornucopian | An individual at the extreme technocentric end of the spectrum, holding strong optimism in human ingenuity and the limitlessness of resources. |
| Carbon capture and storage (CCS) | A technocentric technology that captures CO₂ from power plants or industrial processes and stores it underground to prevent atmospheric release. |
